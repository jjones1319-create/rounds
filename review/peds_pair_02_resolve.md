## B4-Q03 — 3-year-old girl, febrile convulsive status epilepticus, 26 minutes

**First answer (exam pass): B** (intravenous levetiracetam)

### Expert pass

What the findings show. Generalized convulsive seizure, now 26 minutes long, in a febrile 3-year-old with a viral
prodrome and a prior simple febrile seizure. This is febrile status epilepticus (convulsive status is defined
operationally at 5 minutes; 26 minutes is well past). She has received two benzodiazepine doses:

1. Midazolam 5 mg IM by paramedics (16 minutes ago, at ~10 minutes into the seizure). 5 mg is the standard IM dose
   for a child 13–40 kg (RAMPART; AES 2016 algorithm), so this counts as a full first benzodiazepine dose. It
   produced a transient, incomplete response.
2. Lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg IV (1.4 mg, correct dose and under the 4 mg cap), given 6 minutes ago, enough time to judge
   a response (IV lorazepam acts within 2–3 minutes; response is assessed at ~5 minutes). No response.

So the seizure is benzodiazepine-refractory ("established" status epilepticus) and the clock is at 26 minutes,
inside the 20–40 minute window of the AES algorithm's second-therapy phase. The standard next step is a non-
benzodiazepine antiseizure medication: fosphenytoin 20 mg PE/kg, valproate 40 mg/kg, or levetiracetam 60 mg/kg,
which ESETT (adults and children; pediatric subgroup published separately) showed to be equally effective, each
stopping roughly half of benzodiazepine-refractory episodes. Levetiracetam is the only one of the three offered.

Other findings. Glucose 96, so hypoglycemia is excluded. Supple neck, no rash, no bruises, normal skin: nothing
pointing to meningitis, non-accidental injury, or a toxic cause, though an LP and neuroimaging may be considered
after she is stabilized. Eyes deviated to the right with bilateral clonic activity suggests a focal (left-
hemisphere) onset, which is common in febrile status (about a third of FEBSTAT cases had focal features) and does
not change the acute management. Airway: breathing spontaneously with a jaw thrust, SpO2 94% on mask oxygen,
respirations irregular. She is being supported adequately and does not yet need a definitive airway.

Why each option is right or wrong.

- **A. Intubation and propofol infusion.** Third-line (refractory) therapy, reserved for failure of a second-line
  agent, typically beyond 40 minutes. She is oxygenating with basic airway support. Propofol infusion in a small
  child also carries the risk of propofol infusion syndrome. Premature and harmful here.
- **B. IV levetiracetam.** Correct. Second-line agent after two benzodiazepine doses; equal to fosphenytoin and
  valproate in ESETT; no respiratory depression, no cardiac monitoring need, no hepatic concerns in a 3-year-old.
- **C. IV lorazepam.** A third benzodiazepine dose. Guidelines allow a benzodiazepine to be repeated once; she has
  had two full doses, and additional doses add respiratory depression (she already has irregular breathing and
  needs a jaw thrust) with falling likelihood of benefit. Wrong.
- **D. IV phenobarbital.** A legitimate second-line alternative, but in the AES algorithm it is the fallback "if
  none of the three recommended options is available," and it compounds benzodiazepine respiratory depression and
  sedation in a child already needing airway support. Less appropriate than B when levetiracetam is available.
- **E. Rectal diazepam.** Another benzodiazepine, by an inferior route when an IV line is already in place. Wrong.

**Final answer: B.** Confidence: **sure**. Felt difficulty: **★★★** (benzodiazepine-refractory status after two
doses leads directly to the standard second-line step). A ★★★★ rating is defensible: the detail that the
paramedics' IM midazolam counts as a full first dose is what flips the tempting "give another lorazepam" answer.

### Objections

1. No second defensible answer. Phenobarbital (D) is the only other guideline-listed second-line drug, and the
   AES wording ("if none of the above are available") plus the respiratory-depression stacking makes B clearly
   better. If the key were D, I would dispute it.
2. Missing/ambiguous information: none that matters. The stem gives the timeline, both doses with weights, and the
   response to each. It is somewhat hand-holding ("(0.1 mg/kg)", "26 minutes after the seizure began"), which an
   NBME editor might trim, but it does not give the answer away.
3. Strength of the recommendation: strong and current (AES 2016 guideline; ESETT 2019 and its pediatric analysis;
   NICE 2022 and APLS likewise list levetiracetam as a second-line agent). Not contested.
4. Option cues: three options start with "Intravenous," so the trio B/C/D shares the most; the answer lies within
   it, which does not single it out. A is the longest option and is wrong. No grammatical cue.
5. Plausibility: 14.0 kg (30 lb 14 oz; 14.0 × 2.2046 = 30.86 lb = 30 lb 14 oz, consistent) is about the 50th
   percentile for a 3-year-old girl. Temperature 39.4 rectal, pulse 134, respirations 28 and irregular, BP 98/60,
   4 mm reactive pupils: all plausible for a febrile 3-year-old in convulsive status. Midazolam 5 mg IM is the
   correct 13–40 kg dose; lorazepam 1.4 mg is 0.1 mg/kg. Nothing implausible.
6. Resemblance to published questions: the status epilepticus escalation sequence is a staple topic, but I do not
   recognize this stem or its specific details from any published item.
7. Attending/NBME flags: minor only. (a) The right-eye deviation implies a focal onset; a reviewer might ask whether
   the explanation addresses that this does not change acute management and that neuroimaging is worth
   considering later. (b) Meningitis in a febrile 3-year-old with a prolonged seizure: the supple neck and normal
   skin are reassuring, and LP is appropriately deferred until the seizure is controlled; the explanation should
   say so. Neither affects the keyed answer.

## B4-Q04 — 13-year-old boy, stage 1 blood pressure on three visits

**First answer (exam pass): A** (ambulatory blood pressure monitoring)

### Expert pass

What the findings show. He is 13, so by the 2017 AAP Clinical Practice Guideline the adult cutoffs apply
(elevated 120–129/<80; stage 1 hypertension 130–139/80–89; stage 2 ≥140/90). Every properly measured office
reading over 4 months (134/84; 136/86 and 134/84; 132/86 and 134/84) is in the stage 1 range on three separate
visits. He is asymptomatic, with no secondary-cause clues: normal fundi and thyroid, no murmur, strong symmetric
femoral pulses, leg systolic pressure 12 mm Hg above the arm (normal; coarctation gives a lower leg pressure),
no bruit, no cushingoid signs, no acanthosis, normal urinalysis, electrolytes, creatinine, and lipids. Overweight
(BMI 91st percentile), a father with hypertension at 42, normal Tanner 3 puberty: this is the profile of primary
hypertension in an adolescent.

The AAP algorithm for stage 1 BP: repeat in 1–2 weeks (done); if still stage 1, measure upper- and lower-limb BP
(done), counsel on lifestyle, and recheck in 3 months (done, at about 3.5 months); if still stage 1 at that third
visit, obtain ambulatory BP monitoring to confirm the diagnosis and exclude white-coat hypertension, complete the
diagnostic evaluation, continue treatment (lifestyle), and consider referral. Key Action Statement 4 states that
ABPM should be performed to confirm hypertension in a child with stage 1 office readings on 3 separate visits.
That is exactly where he is, so ABPM is the next step.

Why each option is right or wrong.

- **A. Ambulatory BP monitoring.** Correct; the confirmation step after stage 1 office readings on three visits.
  White-coat hypertension is common in adolescents, and the AAP requires confirmation before the diagnosis is
  made and before medication or target-organ assessment.
- **B. Echocardiography.** The AAP recommends echocardiography to assess for left ventricular hypertrophy at the
  time pharmacologic treatment is being considered, not routinely at diagnosis, and the diagnosis has not yet been
  confirmed. Wrong at this step.
- **C. Lisinopril.** Medication is started after confirmed hypertension that persists despite lifestyle
  modification (or for stage 2, symptomatic hypertension, or LVH). He has unconfirmed stage 1 hypertension with
  only 4 months of lifestyle change and no target-organ damage. Wrong.
- **D. Renal ultrasonography.** Indicated in children under 6 with hypertension and in those with an abnormal
  urinalysis or renal function. He is 13 with normal urinalysis and creatinine and no secondary-cause features.
  Wrong.
- **E. Repeat measurement in 6 months.** That is the interval for the elevated-BP pathway. He has met the
  threshold for confirmation now; waiting 6 more months delays diagnosis and treatment. Wrong (and the "harm"
  option).

**Final answer: A.** Confidence: **sure**. Felt difficulty: **★★★** (stage 1 readings on three visits lead to the
standard confirmation step).

### Objections

1. No second defensible answer. The only candidate is B (echocardiography), which some solvers might choose because
   the third-visit box in the AAP algorithm also says "diagnostic evaluation," but echocardiography is explicitly
   tied to the consideration of drug therapy, and ABPM precedes it. A reviewer who thinks the "diagnostic
   evaluation" already happened (labs at visit 2) might lean toward echo, so the explanation should say clearly why
   echo waits for ABPM confirmation and the decision to medicate.
2. Missing/ambiguous information: none. The stem supplies the cuff/auscultation method, rest period, repeat readings,
   both arms and a leg, labs, growth percentiles, and family history.
3. Strength of the recommendation: the 2017 AAP guideline is the current US pediatric standard; the ≥13 adult
   thresholds match the 2017 ACC/AHA adult guideline; the 2022 AHA statement on pediatric ABPM reinforces ABPM for
   confirmation. One thing to verify in Part 2: whether a newer AAP revision has been published since my
   knowledge, since a threshold change would alter the staging (the readings are still above the 130/80 cutoff by
   a comfortable margin, so even a modest revision would be unlikely to change the answer).
4. Option cues: E is the longest option and is wrong; A–D are all short, parallel noun phrases. No giveaway.
5. Plausibility: BMI 23.2 for a 13-year-old boy sits near the 90th–91st percentile on the CDC chart (85th ≈ 22,
   95th ≈ 25), consistent with the stated 91st; Tanner 3 at 13 is normal; pulse 72 at rest is normal; a leg
   systolic 12 mm Hg above the arm with a lower diastolic is a normal pattern. Nothing implausible.
6. Resemblance to published questions: the "stage 1 on three visits → ABPM" pattern is a standard teaching point;
   I do not recognize this specific stem.
7. Attending/NBME flags: minor. (a) The recheck interval is ~3.5 months rather than the AAP's 3 months; harmless.
   (b) The explanation should note that weight management and lifestyle treatment continue in parallel with ABPM,
   and that ABPM in a 13-year-old is interpreted with pediatric ABPM norms (2022 AHA statement). Neither affects
   the keyed answer.
