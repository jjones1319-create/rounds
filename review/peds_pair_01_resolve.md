## B4-Q01 — 14-day-old, rectal temperature 38.4 C, well-appearing, normal inflammatory markers

**Exam pass (first answer, ~90 s):** D. Lumbar puncture and parenteral antibiotics.

**Expert pass**

What the findings show: a 14-day-old with a documented rectal temperature ≥38.0 C at home and in the ED. He looks
well (alert, vigorous cry, consolable, flat fontanelle, CRT <2 s, feeding and voiding normally). Maternal and birth
risk factors are absent (GBS negative, no intrapartum fever, no maternal HSV history, term, discharged day 2, no
vesicles). Urinalysis is negative. The inflammatory markers are all below the usual thresholds (ANC 3,400 <4,000/µL,
CRP 0.6 mg/dL = 6 mg/L <20 mg/L, temperature 38.4 <38.5 C); procalcitonin was not measured. A sibling URI and mild
rhinorrhea suggest a viral source.

Diagnosis: fever without a source in a neonate aged 8–21 days.

Decision: in the current AAP febrile-infant guideline (well-appearing infants 8–60 days, 2021), the 8–21-day bracket
is managed on age alone: urine, blood and CSF cultures, parenteral antibiotics, and hospital monitoring. Inflammatory
markers may be drawn but do not change management in this bracket; a viral source does not either. The age-based
stratification that lets normal markers avert an LP begins at 22 days. So the normal CBC/CRP and the sibling's cold
are distractors, and the answer is the full evaluation with empiric antibiotics.

Why each option is right or wrong:
- A. Discharge with 24-h reassessment: never an option at ≤21 days; bacteremia/meningitis risk is highest in this
  bracket and cannot be excluded by markers or appearance.
- B. Hospital observation without antibiotics: a permitted branch only at 22–28 days (normal markers, CSF not
  obtained); not at 8–21 days.
- C. IM ceftriaxone and discharge: the 29–60-day pathway (and only after CSF or with normal markers); also a neonate
  would ordinarily get ampicillin plus gentamicin or cefotaxime, not ceftriaxone, and would not be sent home.
- D. LP and parenteral antibiotics: correct — CSF before antibiotics, then empiric ampicillin + an aminoglycoside or
  third-generation cephalosporin, admitted.
- E. Serum procalcitonin: would be the natural "next step" in a 22–60-day-old whose risk stratification is pending,
  but at 14 days a normal result would not change management, so it is not the next step.

**Final answer:** D   **Confidence:** sure   **Felt difficulty:** ★★★★ (the normal markers, negative UA and obvious
viral contact pull a solver toward B or E; the single detail that flips it is the age, 14 days, below the 22-day
line).

**Objections**

1. None that holds against the key. Second defensible answer: none under the 2021 AAP guideline. Older risk-stratification
   rules (Rochester) did allow low-risk neonates to be observed, so a solver trained on pre-2021 material might pick B;
   the explanation should name the AAP guideline and the 8–21-day bracket explicitly so the reasoning is unambiguous.
2. Minor realism note, not disqualifying: a net gain of 400 g by day 14 (3.4 → 3.8 kg) is brisk for a breastfed
   newborn (regains birth weight at 10–14 days, then ~20–30 g/day); 3.5–3.6 kg would read more naturally. It does not
   affect the answer and if anything underlines that he is feeding well.
3. Option D does not mention admission or HSV evaluation. Admission is implicit in "parenteral antibiotics" for a
   neonate; HSV testing/acyclovir is driven by risk factors, which the stem has carefully excluded (no maternal
   history, no vesicles, no seizures, normal count, no transaminases given). Acceptable as written.
4. No option cue: lengths are similar, grammar parallel, no repeated word that marks the answer.
5. Vitals and labs are plausible for a febrile 2-week-old (HR 158, RR 44, SpO2 98%, WBC 10.2, ANC 3.4, platelets 312).
6. No resemblance to a specific published item that I recognize; this is the generic "well-appearing febrile neonate
   with normal markers" teaching case.

## B4-Q02 — 8-month-old, intermittent colicky episodes, lethargy, RUQ tubular mass, occult-blood-positive mucoid stool

**Exam pass (first answer, ~90 s):** B. Abdominal ultrasonography.

**Expert pass**

What the findings show: an infant in the peak age range (6–36 months) with paroxysms of inconsolable crying and
knees drawn up every 15–20 minutes, listless/drowsy between episodes, non-bilious vomiting, a viral illness the week
before (mesenteric lymphoid hyperplasia as the lead point), a sausage-shaped tender mass in the right upper quadrant
with an "empty" right lower quadrant (Dance sign), and mucoid stool with occult blood (early "currant jelly").
She is hemodynamically stable (BP 92/58, CRT 2 s, warm, moist membranes) and has no peritoneal signs (soft, not
distended, no guarding or rebound). Afebrile, soft flat fontanelle.

Diagnosis: ileocolic intussusception, until proven otherwise.

Decision: the diagnosis is clinical-plus-imaging. Abdominal ultrasound is the diagnostic test of choice (target/
doughnut sign on transverse, pseudokidney on longitudinal; sensitivity and specificity near 100% in experienced
hands, no radiation), and it also shows free fluid, a pathologic lead point and bowel perfusion. Once confirmed, and
with no perforation or peritonitis, non-operative reduction by air (pneumatic) enema under fluoroscopy or
ultrasound guidance follows, with surgery on standby. Because imaging has not yet been done and the mass is
"ill-defined," the next step is to confirm with ultrasound, not to proceed straight to the enema.

Why each option is right or wrong:
- A. Abdominal radiography: insensitive and nonspecific for intussusception; its only role is to look for free air
  or obstruction, which ultrasound and the clinical examination already address here. Not the best next step.
- B. Abdominal ultrasonography: correct — the first-line confirmatory study in a stable infant with suspected
  intussusception.
- C. Air-contrast enema: the treatment, done after ultrasound confirmation; it is contraindicated with perforation or
  peritonitis, which is why confirmation and a check for free fluid/free air precede it. Tempting because the clinical
  picture is so typical.
- D. CT with IV contrast: radiation and sedation in an infant with a diagnosis ultrasound makes reliably; not
  indicated.
- E. Lumbar puncture: the lethargy between episodes is a known intussusception presentation ("neurologic" or
  "painless" intussusception), not meningitis; she is afebrile with a soft, flat fontanelle and an abdominal mass.

**Final answer:** B   **Confidence:** likely (sure on the diagnosis; "likely" only because C is the second answer a
strong solver could argue, see objection 1)   **Felt difficulty:** ★★★ (the sausage mass plus bloody mucoid stool
leads straight to the standard confirmatory test; lethargy → LP and "it's obvious, just reduce it" → enema are the
distractors).

**Objections**

1. Second defensible answer: C. In a classic presentation some surgeons and older teaching go directly to the air
   enema as "diagnostic and therapeutic," and older exam items keyed it that way. Current practice (ACR Appropriateness
   Criteria, AAP/pediatric surgery teaching) is ultrasound first, then enema, and the item helps itself by (a) not
   having done any imaging, (b) making the mass "ill-defined" and the blood only occult, and (c) asking for the next
   step rather than the definitive treatment. I think the ultrasound key holds, but the explanation must state plainly
   why the enema is not the *next* step (confirm the diagnosis, exclude peritonitis/free fluid, then reduce) and cite
   a current source, or an NBME reviewer will flag the item as having two acceptable answers.
2. Resuscitation before imaging: a drowsy infant who has vomited twice should get IV access and fluids while being
   imaged; no option offers this, so it does not compete, but the explanation could note it (ultrasound at the bedside
   does not delay it).
3. Vitals: HR 148 is at the upper end for a crying/painful 8-month-old; BP 92/58 and RR 36 are normal; 8.0 kg is about
   the 50th percentile for an 8-month-old girl; T 37.6 is afebrile. All plausible.
4. Wording: "air-contrast enema" is the NBME phrasing for pneumatic reduction; pediatric radiology usually says "air
   enema." Acceptable. No option-length or grammar cue (the longest option, D, is a distractor).
5. The stem says "cries when her abdomen is palpated" then "mildly tender mass" with "no guarding" — consistent, fine.
6. No resemblance to a specific published question that I recognize; it is the standard intussusception vignette.
