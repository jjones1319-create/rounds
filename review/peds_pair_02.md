### B4-Q03  ·  ★★★★ Above standard

*System:* Nervous System & Special Senses · *Discipline:* Pediatrics / Emergency Medicine

*Topic:* Convulsive status epilepticus continuing after two adequate benzodiazepine doses: a second-line agent, not a third benzodiazepine

A 3-year-old girl is brought to the emergency department by ambulance because of a generalized seizure that began 20 minutes ago. Her father says she has had a runny nose and fever since yesterday; she was playing when her eyes rolled upward and all four limbs began to jerk, and she has not responded since. Paramedics gave 5 mg of midazolam intramuscularly 10 minutes ago; the jerking slowed for about a minute and then resumed. At 18 months of age she had a 3-minute seizure with a fever and recovered fully. She was born at term, has no other medical history, takes no medications, and her immunizations are up to date.

She weighs 14.0 kg (30 lb 14 oz). Temperature is 39.4 C (102.9 F) rectally, pulse is 134/min, respirations are 28/min and irregular, blood pressure is 98/60 mm Hg, and pulse oximetry shows 95% while she receives oxygen by face mask. Rhythmic clonic movements of all four limbs continue, and both eyes are deviated to the right. The pupils are 4 mm and reactive. The neck is supple. There is clear nasal discharge; the tympanic membranes, lungs, and skin are normal, with no rash or bruises. Point-of-care glucose is 96 mg/dL. An intravenous line is placed, blood is drawn, and lorazepam 1.4 mg (0.1 mg/kg) is given intravenously.

Six minutes later, 26 minutes after the seizure began, the clonic movements are unchanged. She is breathing spontaneously with a jaw thrust, and pulse oximetry shows 94%.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?**

- **A.** Endotracheal intubation and propofol infusion
- **B.** Intravenous levetiracetam
- **C.** Intravenous lorazepam
- **D.** Intravenous phenobarbital
- **E.** Rectal diazepam

**Answer: B. Intravenous levetiracetam**

*Why it is correct*

This child is in **convulsive status epilepticus**: a generalized convulsive seizure lasting more than 5 minutes, here 26 minutes and counting, most likely febrile status epilepticus given the fever, the viral prodrome, the earlier febrile seizure, the supple neck, and the normal glucose. The American Epilepsy Society (AES) 2016 guideline organizes treatment by time from seizure onset: a stabilization phase (0 to 5 minutes: airway, oxygen, monitoring, glucose, intravenous access), an initial-therapy phase (5 to 20 minutes) in which a benzodiazepine is the treatment of choice (level A), a second-therapy phase (20 to 40 minutes), and a third-therapy phase (40 to 60 minutes). She has received two adequate benzodiazepine doses and the seizure has passed the 20-minute mark, so the next drug is a **second-line agent**: intravenous levetiracetam 60 mg/kg (maximum 4,500 mg), fosphenytoin 20 mg PE/kg (maximum 1,500 mg PE), or valproate 40 mg/kg (maximum 3,000 mg), given as a single full dose.

Both doses she received were full doses. The midazolam dose follows the RAMPART protocol (5 mg intramuscularly for children weighing 13 to 40 kg; here 5 / 14.0 = 0.36 mg/kg), and the lorazepam dose is 0.1 mg/kg (14.0 × 0.1 = 1.4 mg; maximum 4 mg per dose). Both reach the minimum adequate doses that defined benzodiazepine-refractory status epilepticus in the ESETT trial for children under 32 kg (midazolam 0.3 mg/kg intramuscularly, that is 4.2 mg for her, or lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg intravenously). The AES guideline allows intravenous lorazepam or diazepam to be repeated once at full dose and says that initial therapies should not otherwise be given twice; after two doses, more benzodiazepine adds respiratory depression without adding seizure control. In the North London population-based study of childhood convulsive status epilepticus, treatment with more than two benzodiazepine doses was associated with respiratory depression (odds ratio 2.9) and with episodes lasting more than 60 minutes (odds ratio 3.6).

ESETT (the Established Status Epilepticus Treatment Trial, 2019) randomized 384 children and adults whose convulsive status epilepticus continued after adequate benzodiazepine to levetiracetam, fosphenytoin, or valproate: seizures stopped with improving responsiveness at 60 minutes in 47%, 45%, and 46%, with no significant difference in life-threatening hypotension, intubation, or death in the trial overall; among the 225 children (the trial's group under 18 years) the rates were 52%, 49%, and 52%, and the one safety difference in children was more intubation within 60 minutes with fosphenytoin (33%, against 8% with levetiracetam and 11% with valproate). The AES guideline, which predates ESETT, already treated the three as equivalent choices with no evidence that one is better (level U for fosphenytoin and levetiracetam, level B for valproate), so any one is acceptable, and levetiracetam is the one offered here. For 14.0 kg: levetiracetam 60 × 14.0 = 840 mg intravenously, infused over 10 minutes as in ESETT; the alternatives would be fosphenytoin 20 × 14.0 = 280 mg PE or valproate 40 × 14.0 = 560 mg. Care continues in parallel: airway positioning and oxygen, cardiorespiratory monitoring, a search for the cause (electrolytes now; because of the fever, consideration of meningitis or encephalitis once the seizure stops, with lumbar puncture and empiric antimicrobials if the examination afterward is not reassuring), and preparation for the third phase (a repeat second-line agent or an anesthetic infusion with continuous EEG monitoring in an intensive care unit) if she is still seizing at 40 minutes.

*Why the other choices are wrong*

- **A.** Anesthetic infusions (propofol, midazolam, pentobarbital, or thiopental) with continuous EEG monitoring belong to the third-therapy phase, for seizures that continue after an adequate second-line agent (refractory status epilepticus, from about 40 minutes), or whenever the airway or breathing fails. She is breathing spontaneously with an oxygen saturation of 94% to 95%, and the second-line drug has not yet been tried; intubation and propofol now expose her to hypotension and a prolonged intensive care stay before the drug that stops the seizure in about half of such children has been given. *(too aggressive / wrong sequence (third-phase therapy before the second-line agent))*
- **C.** A third benzodiazepine dose is the tempting error. The AES guideline permits one repeat of intravenous lorazepam or diazepam at full dose, and she has already had two adequate benzodiazepine doses (the midazolam counts); the initial-therapy phase ends at 20 minutes. Beyond two doses, benzodiazepines add respiratory depression (odds ratio 2.9 in the North London study) and are associated with seizures that last more than 60 minutes. A second lorazepam dose would be right if only one adequate dose had been given and the seizure were still in its first 20 minutes. *(right drug class, wrong time (a third benzodiazepine after two full doses and 20 minutes))*
- **D.** Phenobarbital (15 mg/kg intravenously, here 15 × 14.0 = 210 mg) is the AES alternative in both phases when the preferred drugs are not available: as initial therapy if no benzodiazepine can be given (level A) and as second therapy if fosphenytoin, valproate, and levetiracetam are all unavailable (level B). It causes more sedation and respiratory depression, which would compound two benzodiazepine doses, and the three preferred agents work in about half of children. *(correct for a different situation (when the three recommended second-line agents are unavailable))*
- **E.** Rectal diazepam (0.2 to 0.5 mg/kg, maximum 20 mg) is a level B initial-therapy alternative for a patient without intravenous access, typically given at home or before hospital arrival. It is another benzodiazepine, she has an intravenous line, and she has already received two adequate doses; it would add respiratory depression and delay the second-line agent. *(wrong route and wrong sequence (an initial-therapy alternative in a patient with intravenous access who has had two doses))*

*Educational objective:* In convulsive status epilepticus a benzodiazepine is first-line and may be repeated once; when the seizure continues after two adequate doses and 20 minutes, give a single full dose of a second-line agent (levetiracetam 60 mg/kg, fosphenytoin 20 mg PE/kg, or valproate 40 mg/kg, about equally effective in ESETT) rather than a third benzodiazepine, which adds respiratory depression without seizure control.

### B4-Q04  ·  ★★★ Board standard

*System:* Cardiovascular System · *Discipline:* Pediatrics

*Topic:* Stage 1 office blood pressures on three visits in a 13-year-old: confirm with ambulatory blood pressure monitoring before the diagnosis is acted on

A 13-year-old boy is brought to the office by his mother for a scheduled blood pressure recheck. At his well-child examination 4 months ago his blood pressure was 134/84 mm Hg; at a return visit 2 weeks after that it was 136/86 mm Hg in the right arm, 134/84 mm Hg in the left arm, and 148/82 mm Hg in the right leg. Each reading was obtained by auscultation with an appropriately sized cuff after 5 minutes of seated rest. He has had no headaches, visual changes, chest pain, palpitations, snoring, muscle cramps, or flushing. He takes no medications, supplements, energy drinks, or other substances, was born at term, and has never been hospitalized. His father was diagnosed with hypertension at 42 years of age; there is no family history of kidney disease or early cardiovascular death. Since the first visit he has stopped drinking soda and plays basketball three afternoons a week. Urinalysis and serum electrolyte, creatinine, and lipid values obtained at the second visit were normal.

He is at the 58th percentile for height and the 87th percentile for weight; BMI is 23.2 kg/m² (91st percentile). Blood pressure today, by auscultation in the right arm after 5 minutes of rest, is 132/86 mm Hg, and a repeat measurement 5 minutes later is 134/84 mm Hg; pulse is 72/min. Fundoscopic examination and the thyroid gland are normal. Heart sounds are normal without murmurs, and femoral pulses are strong and symmetric. There is no abdominal bruit, acanthosis nigricans, striae, or edema. Sexual maturity rating is Tanner stage 3.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?**

- **A.** Ambulatory blood pressure monitoring
- **B.** Echocardiography
- **C.** Lisinopril therapy
- **D.** Renal ultrasonography
- **E.** Repeat blood pressure measurement in 6 months

**Answer: A. Ambulatory blood pressure monitoring**

*Why it is correct*

For adolescents 13 years and older the 2017 American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) clinical practice guideline uses the adult thresholds (Table 3): normal below 120/80 mm Hg, elevated blood pressure 120 to 129 with a diastolic pressure below 80, **stage 1 hypertension 130/80 to 139/89**, and stage 2 at 140/90 or higher. Every one of his auscultatory readings over three visits (134/84; 136/86 and 134/84; 132/86 and 134/84 today) sits in the stage 1 range. The guideline's diagnostic rule is that hypertension is diagnosed from auscultatory-confirmed readings at or above the threshold on 3 different visits (Key Action Statement [KAS] 3; grade C, moderate), and its stage 1 pathway is: lifestyle counseling and a recheck in 1 to 2 weeks; if still stage 1, arm and leg pressures and a recheck in 3 months; and if still stage 1 at the third visit, **ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM)**, the diagnostic evaluation, and treatment (section 4.3c).

The step this third visit calls for is therefore ABPM: 'ABPM should be performed for the confirmation of hypertension in children and adolescents with office blood pressure measurements in the elevated blood pressure category for 1 year or more or with stage 1 hypertension over 3 clinic visits' (KAS 6; grade C, moderate recommendation), and the management table adds that ABPM is done to confirm hypertension before the diagnostic evaluation is begun (Table 11). A 24-hour recording with a validated monitor separates sustained hypertension from white coat hypertension, which the guideline estimates in up to half of children evaluated for elevated office readings and which otherwise leads to needless testing and medication, and it detects masked and nocturnal hypertension. The 2022 American Heart Association scientific statement on pediatric ABPM makes the same point, suggesting that ABPM be used to confirm the diagnosis before antihypertensive medication is started, and it classifies an adolescent's recording as hypertensive when the 24-hour mean is 125/75 mm Hg or higher, the wake mean 130/80 or higher, or the sleep mean 110/65 or higher.

Nothing here demands a different first move. His leg pressure exceeds his arm pressures, so there is no coarctation gradient; the femoral pulses are normal; there is no bruit, no hypokalemia, no abnormal urinalysis or creatinine, and no sign of thyroid, adrenal, or other endocrine disease. He is overweight with a hypertensive father: for a child 6 years or older with a positive family history, overweight or obesity, and no history or examination finding suggesting a secondary cause, an extensive evaluation for secondary hypertension is not required (KAS 11; grade C, moderate). The screening tests for all patients (urinalysis, chemistry panel, lipid profile; Table 10) have been done. If ABPM confirms hypertension, management continues with lifestyle change (weight management, diet, and activity), and echocardiography and a drug follow only when pharmacologic treatment is being considered.

*Why the other choices are wrong*

- **B.** Echocardiography is recommended to assess for cardiac target-organ damage (left ventricular mass, geometry, and function) at the time pharmacologic treatment of hypertension is being considered (KAS 15; grade C, moderate), and electrocardiography is not to be used for that purpose (KAS 14). The diagnosis is unconfirmed and no drug is planned, so it is premature; it is the right step once ABPM confirms hypertension and medication is contemplated. *(wrong sequence (target-organ assessment before the diagnosis is confirmed and before drugs are considered))*
- **C.** Pharmacologic treatment (an ACE inhibitor, angiotensin receptor blocker, long-acting calcium channel blocker, or thiazide diuretic) is for children and adolescents with hypertension who have failed lifestyle modification, particularly those with left ventricular hypertrophy, symptoms, or stage 2 hypertension without a clearly modifiable factor (KAS 21; grade B, moderate). He has asymptomatic stage 1 readings, has changed his diet and activity for only 4 months, and does not yet have a confirmed diagnosis; lisinopril now risks years of treatment for white coat hypertension. *(too aggressive (drug therapy before confirmation and before lifestyle treatment has failed))*
- **D.** Renal ultrasonography is a screening test for children younger than 6 years and for those with an abnormal urinalysis or renal function (Table 10), and Doppler renal ultrasonography may screen for renal artery stenosis in normal-weight children 8 years and older in whom renovascular hypertension is suspected (KAS 16; grade C): stage 2 or marked diastolic hypertension, hypokalemia, discrepant kidney sizes, or an abdominal bruit. He has none of these, he is overweight (Doppler studies perform poorly in overweight children, which is why KAS 16 is limited to normal weight), and his urinalysis and creatinine are normal. *(correct for a different age group or finding (under 6 years, abnormal urinalysis or renal function, or features of renovascular disease))*
- **E.** A recheck in 6 months is the pathway for elevated blood pressure (120 to 129 with a diastolic pressure below 80 at his age): lifestyle advice and a repeat measurement in 6 months, with ABPM only if readings remain elevated after 12 months (section 4.3b). His readings are stage 1 hypertension on three visits over 4 months, which the guideline says to confirm now; another 6 months leaves possible sustained hypertension unconfirmed and untreated. *(too passive / correct for a different category (the elevated-blood-pressure pathway))*

*Educational objective:* In a child 6 years or older, stage 1 office hypertension (130/80 to 139/89 mm Hg from age 13) on three visits is confirmed with ambulatory blood pressure monitoring before the diagnosis is acted on; echocardiography comes when drug therapy is being considered, drugs after lifestyle change fails (or for stage 2, symptoms, or left ventricular hypertrophy), and renal imaging only for children under 6, an abnormal urinalysis or renal function, or signs of renovascular disease.

Pediatrics. 2017;140(3):e20171904 (Table 3; sections 4.3b, 4.3c, and 4.7b; Key Action Statements 3, 6, 11, 14, 15, 16, and 21; Table 10); errata Pediatrics. 2017;140(6):e20173035 and 2018;142(3):e20181739.
