## Passage CP-P09: Bomb calorimetry of a meal bar

A bomb calorimeter measures the energy released when a sample burns completely. A weighed sample is sealed in a steel bomb under about 30 atm of oxygen, the bomb is submerged in a known mass of water, and the sample is ignited by a fuse wire. The temperature of the whole assembly (bomb, water and bucket) rises, and the heat released is q = CΔT, where C is the heat capacity of the assembly. C cannot be calculated from the parts with enough accuracy, so it is found by calibration, either by burning a reference material of certified heat of combustion or by delivering a measured quantity of electrical energy from a heater inside the bucket. Because the bomb's volume is fixed, the heat it releases equals the decrease in the internal energy of its contents; for foods the difference between this and the enthalpy change is well under 1% and is ignored.

In a hypothetical study, a nutrition laboratory measured the gross energy of a 50 g meal bar. The assembly was first calibrated electrically: a heater delivering a constant 110 W was switched on at t = 60 s and off at t = 360 s while the temperature was recorded (Figure 1). It was then calibrated chemically by burning a 1.000 g pellet of benzoic acid, a certified reference material with a heat of combustion of 26.4 kJ/g (Table 1). Finally a 1.000 g sample of the dried, ground bar was burned under identical conditions (Table 1). The small corrections for the fuse wire were applied before the values in Table 1 were recorded.

Food labels do not report gross energy. The Atwater general factors (Table 2) estimate the metabolizable energy: the gross energy less the energy lost in faeces and urine. Fat and available carbohydrate are oxidized in the body to the same final products as in the bomb, carbon dioxide and water, so for them only incomplete digestion is deducted. Protein is different: in the bomb its nitrogen ends up mainly as N₂, whereas the body excretes nitrogen chiefly as urea, which still has combustion energy, so protein's gross energy of about 23.6 kJ/g is reduced to 17 kJ/g. Dietary fibre is not digested by human enzymes and is only partly fermented by colonic bacteria. Because enthalpy is a state function, the energy released when glucose is oxidized to carbon dioxide and water is the same whether it burns in the bomb in a fraction of a second or passes through glycolysis, the citric acid cycle and the electron transport chain. To compare pathways, the investigators used rounded standard enthalpies of combustion of −2,800 kJ/mol for glucose and −1,360 kJ/mol for lactic acid.

## Passage CP-P10: Mass spectrometry of a tryptic peptide

In matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI) mass spectrometry, the sample is co-crystallized with a matrix, an aromatic acid that absorbs the laser's wavelength. A laser pulse vaporizes a little of the crystal, and the matrix transfers a proton to most sample molecules, giving [M + H]⁺ ions; a few molecules gain two protons. The ions are accelerated through a potential difference V, so a singly charged ion acquires kinetic energy eV, where e is the elementary charge, and an ion of charge number z acquires zeV. They then drift down a field-free tube of length L and reach the detector after a time t = L/v, the time of flight. The spectrum plots detector signal against m/z, the mass in daltons (1 Da = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg) divided by the charge number. The most intense peak of a small peptide is its monoisotopic peak, in which every atom is the lightest isotope; the peak one unit higher comes from molecules containing one heavy atom, mostly carbon-13, whose natural abundance the investigators took as 1.1%.

In a hypothetical study, a 21 kDa recombinant protein expressed in insect cells was suspected of carrying a post-translational modification. The protein was digested with trypsin, which cleaves on the carboxyl side of lysine and arginine, and the peptides were analysed by MALDI time-of-flight at V = 20 kV with L = 2.0 m; singly charged ions of m/z 900 reached the detector 30.5 µs after the laser pulse. Five peptides were detected (Figure 1). Four of them, T1, T2, T4 and T5, matched the masses predicted from the gene sequence within 0.1 Da. T3, whose predicted sequence is Ala-Tyr-Ser-Leu-Gln-Asp-Val-Phe-Lys with a predicted [M + H]⁺ of 1,070.55, instead appeared at 1,150.52, a difference of 79.97 Da. Phosphorylation of a serine, threonine or tyrosine side chain adds HPO₃, 79.97 Da, and the investigators adopted this as their working hypothesis.

To locate the modification, T3 ions were selected, fragmented along the peptide backbone, and the fragments were analysed in a second stage. Fragments that retain the C-terminal lysine (the y series) form a ladder in which each member differs from the next by the mass of one amino acid residue (Table 2; residue masses in Table 1). The neutral mass of any peptide is the sum of its residue masses plus the mass of water (18.01 Da) for the two termini, and the [M + H]⁺ ion is one unit heavier still. A second sample of the protein was digested with chymotrypsin, which cleaves on the carboxyl side of phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan, and its peptides were analysed in the same way.

## Passage CP-P11: Presbyopia and reading lenses

The cornea and the lens of the eye together form a real, inverted image on the retina, about 2.0 cm behind the lens. Light is bent at each boundary according to Snell's law, n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂, where the refractive indices are about 1.00 for air, 1.38 for the cornea, 1.34 for the aqueous humour in front of the lens, 1.41 for the lens itself and 1.34 for the vitreous humour behind it. The power of a lens is P = 1/f, in diopters (D) when the focal length f is in metres, and the powers of thin lenses in contact add. The relaxed eye has a total power of about 50 D; to focus an object 25 cm away it must reach about 54 D, which it does by accommodation, the ciliary muscle thickening the lens. The far point is the greatest distance at which an object is seen sharply with the eye relaxed (infinity for a normal eye) and the near point the smallest distance at which it is seen sharply with full accommodation. The amplitude of accommodation, in diopters, is 1/(near point) − 1/(far point), with distances in metres. With age the lens stiffens, the amplitude falls and the near point recedes beyond comfortable reading distance: presbyopia.

In a hypothetical clinic study, the near point and far point of three patients were measured without lenses (Table 1). The clinic takes 25 cm as the standard reading distance and treats a spectacle lens as a thin lens placed at the eye, neglecting the small separation between them. Lenses are prescribed with the thin-lens equation 1/dₒ + 1/dᵢ = 1/f, in which a virtual image, formed on the same side of the lens as the object, has a negative image distance. A reading lens must form a virtual image of the page, held at 25 cm, at the patient's near point, where the eye can focus it; a distance lens must form a virtual image of a distant object at the patient's far point.

Patient C, who is myopic, reported reading comfortably without spectacles but seeing distant objects blurred.

| Patient | Age (y) | Near (cm) | Far (cm) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 48 | 50 | ∞ |

## Passage CP-P12: An electrochemical glucose test strip

Glucose oxidase is a flavoprotein oxidoreductase: each subunit holds one tightly bound flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD). It oxidizes β-D-glucose at C1 to D-glucono-1,5-lactone in two half-steps. First, glucose reduces the enzyme's FAD: glucose + GOx·FAD → gluconolactone + GOx·FADH₂. Second, the natural electron acceptor, oxygen, re-oxidizes the flavin: GOx·FADH₂ + O₂ → GOx·FAD + H₂O₂. First-generation glucose sensors oxidized the hydrogen peroxide at a platinum electrode held near +0.6 V against a reference electrode and measured the current, but the reading depended on the oxygen content of the sample, and at that potential ascorbate, urate and acetaminophen in blood are also oxidized.

In a hypothetical second-generation strip, glucose oxidase and a large excess of potassium ferricyanide, K₃Fe(CN)₆, are dried onto a carbon working electrode. A 1 µL drop of blood rehydrates the layer. Glucose reduces the enzyme's FAD as before, but ferricyanide now takes the electrons: GOx·FADH₂ + 2 Fe(CN)₆³⁻ → GOx·FAD + 2 Fe(CN)₆⁴⁻ + 2 H⁺. After a 5.0 s incubation the meter applies +0.3 V between the working electrode and the reference electrode, re-oxidizing the ferrocyanide formed, Fe(CN)₆⁴⁻ → Fe(CN)₆³⁻ + e⁻, and records the current for 5.0 s; each glucose molecule therefore delivers two electrons to the electrode. For a calibration sample the average current over the measurement was 10 µA. Table 1 lists the standard reduction potentials at pH 7 that the investigators used; for FAD they used the value for the free coenzyme, noting that the potential of enzyme-bound FAD differs.

The investigators also made open-circuit measurements, in which no current flows and the electrode potential follows the Nernst equation for the ferricyanide/ferrocyanide couple: E = E°′ − (0.0592 V) log([Fe(CN)₆⁴⁻]/[Fe(CN)₆³⁻]). In a fresh strip the ferrocyanide:ferricyanide ratio is about 1:100. Dissolved oxygen can still compete with ferricyanide for the reduced enzyme, diverting electrons into hydrogen peroxide, which is not oxidized at +0.3 V; the ferricyanide is loaded in excess so that it outcompetes oxygen, and the investigators planned a test of the residual interference. Venous blood carries less oxygen than arterial blood, and capillary blood from a fingertip lies between the two.

| FAD + 2 H⁺ + 2 e⁻ → FADH₂ | −0.22 |
